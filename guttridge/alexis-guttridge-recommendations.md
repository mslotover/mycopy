# Alexis Guttridge: Hospitality Recommendations

*A summary of the advice in her Instagram posts (@alexis_guttridge), drawn mainly from May to November 2026.*

## Who she is

Alexis is a former Boston restaurateur (West Bridge). She has 20+ years of experience, sold her business at 29 and now lives in South Australia. She runs ANCHR Consulting, advising restaurants, hotels and wineries on guest experience, team culture and leadership. She also hosts the *Determined AF* podcast and speaks at industry events (for example Balancing Plates, McLaren Vale). Earlier posts (2023 to 2025) were life and business coaching for women. Her hospitality content, and the audience that came with it, began around late May 2026, and that is what this summary covers.

Her core belief appears in many posts: **guests rarely remember exactly what they ate, but they always remember how the place made them feel.**

## 1. The first 30 seconds decide the visit

- **Acknowledge every guest at the door**, even if you can't help them yet. "Hey, we'll be right with you" changes how welcome people feel. Guests left standing and unsure whether anyone noticed is, in her words, one of the most heartbreaking small moments.
- **Treat the greeting as the first course.** Your first hello has more influence than most owners realise. A warm welcome is visible: guests' shoulders drop.
- **Look at the experience before arrival.** Does anyone answer the phone? Is the booking easy? Does the outside make it obvious where to go and whether you're open? Does your Instagram match the room guests walk into? She wants the promise made online to match what people find in person.
- **Reduce confusion.** "Busy doesn't make people uncomfortable. Confusion does." Don't point, walk people there. Spot confused guests before they ask for help.
- **Give people a minute to settle** before launching into water, specials and "Have you dined with us before?" Offer somewhere to put bags, coats and flowers.

## 2. Notice the small details

Her recurring theme is that great hospitality is hundreds of tiny moments, and most cost nothing.

- **Anticipate needs:** a shell bowl with the prawns, napkins with the ribs, a steak knife before the steak. Clear space on the table before the next dish arrives.
- **Never let a glass sit empty** or a table go uncleared. A great bartender spots a nearly empty glass with a nod, not a wave.
- **Glassware and cutlery matter.** Water spots, a glass still hot from the dishwasher and poor cutlery all register with guests.
- **Make information travel with the guest.** The allergy, the birthday and the early finish should never need repeating. "A guest shouldn't have to tell their story three times."
- **Let each table set its own pace.** Some want to linger, others have a show to catch.
- **Remember something personal**, such as the holiday they mentioned or the dog, rather than just their drink order.
- **Say goodbye as warmly as hello.** The last 30 seconds matter. Use a thank-you, eye contact and a name, not a cheesy script.
- **Check the room itself:** sound levels (can people hear each other?), lighting, music, bathrooms, the "table nobody wants" and the menu's clarity. Make someone specifically responsible for bathroom checks and table clearing, rather than "someone should".

## 3. Build regulars from the first visit

"Regulars aren't made on the tenth visit. They're made on the first." First-time guests are far more observant than regulars, so use their first impression well. Recommendations:

- Remember names, favourite seats and favourite drinks.
- Make people feel like regulars before they are, through genuine recognition rather than a loyalty scheme.
- Introduce new staff to regulars. Good staff introduce themselves rather than wait to be recognised.
- Treat regulars as people who are home, not VIPs. The best ones don't expect special treatment.
- Look after solo diners and bar guests. A table for one is not a sad table. Check on them once more and offer conversation if they want it.

## 4. Leadership and managers

- **Managers should be on the floor, not behind a laptop.** "You cannot lead hospitality from behind a screen." The best managers run food, clear tables and seat guests.
- **Make managers able to decide without the owner.** Ask "What do you think we should do?" and "Come to me with the problem AND what you think the solution is." Great managers "don't create followers. They create more people who can lead."
- **Test the system:** "What happens to the experience when your best manager isn't there?" If the answer is "it falls apart", that is the first thing to work on.
- **Beware of owner dependence.** She says she over-controlled at West Bridge and that burnout taught her the difference between caring about everything and being involved in everything. "You can give everything to this industry without giving it every single piece of you."
- **Make it safe to admit mistakes.** Teach staff to say "I screwed this up, I know how to fix it, I'm on it." Mistakes get bigger when people are scared to report them.
- **Recognise what's working.** Name good behaviour when you see it, whether that is someone going out of their way for a guest or stepping in to help a teammate. That is how it becomes part of how the restaurant operates, instead of one good employee's habit. Give the team both: "here's what we're doing really well" and "here's where we can get better."
- **Don't carry one difficult guest into the next table.** Fix what you can, learn what's worth learning, then give the next guest the same version of you.

## 5. Pre-shift (lineup)

She treats pre-shift as one of the highest-leverage routines.

- Use it to remind the team what kind of experience they are about to create, not just to pass on specials and bookings.
- Don't turn every lineup into a list of yesterday's mistakes. Include something to feel good about.
- **Teach one thing:** a dish, a wine, how to greet a guest, a question for servers to ask that night.
- **Set the energy.** Think game day. If you can't bring energy that day, let someone else run it.
- **Prepare.** Take five minutes beforehand to decide what you want the team to know, feel or do differently.
- Tell the team what to look for: empty glasses, guests waiting at the door, tables that need clearing.
- Pick one dish or drink and have everyone describe it without reading the menu.
- Ask: "What's one tiny thing we could do tonight that a guest might still remember tomorrow?"

## 6. Team culture and consistency

- **Watch how staff treat each other**, not just how they treat guests. It is one of the fastest ways to read culture. After the last guest leaves, see whether people say goodnight to the kitchen, thank the dishwasher and check on the bartender.
- **Set specific standards.** "Give great service" isn't training. Replace "make guests feel welcome" with "acknowledge every guest who walks through the door, even if you can't help them immediately."
- **Check consistency across shifts and managers.** Pick one piece of the experience (the greeting, check-backs, farewells) and watch it on different shifts.
- **Ask who owns the floor** when service goes sideways, so the strongest person doesn't end up putting out every fire.
- **Spot the person carrying the shift.** One exhausted staff member quietly fixing everyone's mistakes is a warning sign of culture breaking down.
- **Hire for who people are**, not only what they've done. Some of the best hospitality people weren't the most experienced, but they genuinely gave a shit about people. She advises developing natural talent by letting them lead a pre-shift, recommend a wine or own a guest experience, and telling them what they did well and why it mattered.

## 7. Diagnose causes, not symptoms

A frequent message to owners: trace a complaint backwards before fixing it.

| What you hear | Where she'd look first |
|---|---|
| "Staff keep leaving" | Who they work for, how they're managed and their first 30 days |
| "Service is inconsistent" | Whether expectations are clear, whether there is a proper pre-shift, and whether training is followed up |
| "Managers are burning out" | Which decisions they're actually allowed to make |
| "Guests aren't coming back" | The whole guest experience, from arrival to departure and follow-up |
| "The owner can't step away" | Why one person still makes every decision |
| "It feels chaotic" | Reservation clumping, nowhere to wait, door management, server loads, and the manager covering three roles |
| "We need more customers" | Often a better reason for existing guests to return |
| "We think it's staffing" | Often a leadership and communication problem |

**Hidden costs:** lost knowledge when good staff leave, managers fighting fires, repeated conversations and owner dependence all hurt the bottom line without appearing on the P&L.

**For slow nights:** pick one problem, such as dead Tuesdays or occasion-only guests. Ask what would give existing guests a reason to come, and talk to five people who already love you. Stop protecting something just because it used to work.

## 8. How she assesses a venue (steal this)

Her method is a useful checklist for any owner:

1. **Experience it as a guest first.** Call, book online, and check Google, the website and Instagram.
2. **Look for patterns in reviews**, not individual complaints. Six people describing the same frustration over six months is the signal.
3. **Ask people outside the business** what they've heard, or pay a trusted friend to dine and report back.
4. **Have an anonymous dinner** on an ordinary night.
5. **Look upstream.** Slow service, a server who can't answer menu questions and a frantic team may all trace back to training, permissions or systems.
6. **Watch the managers** under pressure. Who does the team look at when things go sideways?
7. **Understand why things are the way they are** before changing anything. She says a venue deserves more than "someone with a clipboard and a list of everything you're doing wrong".

She has built her **Client Audit** around this, and offers a **free 25-area self-assessment tool** (comment "LOOK" on her posts). Her 21-point audit series ends on one question: *"Would I trust you with someone I care about?"*

## 9. Getting fresh eyes on your own venue

If you've walked in 10,000 times, it's hard to see. She suggests asking another owner to walk through and tell you what you're not seeing, visiting somewhere very different, following non-competitors such as wineries and hotels, and asking your team what one thing they'd change. An outside advisor is another option, but the point is the perspective, not who provides it.

## 10. Balance and sustainability

She is open about burnout and argues that the industry's intensity shouldn't cost you your health or your life. Don't let the restaurant always come first, prefer "better, not bigger" growth, and keep learning. She also asks guests to be gracious: tell an owner privately before posting a damaging review, thank staff, stay off your phone when the server arrives, and say specifically what you loved.

## Notes for Amanda

- **Fastest wins, all free:** acknowledge every arrival, tighten the pre-shift (one lesson, one positive, one thing to watch for), assign owners for bathrooms and table clearing, and run an anonymous guest-eyes walk-through of your own venue.
- **Bigger structural work:** manager decision rights, clear written service standards and checking consistency across shifts.
- **Caveat:** this summary is based on post captions. Her audit series and some carousels carried most of their detail in slides and video, which weren't in the scrape, so only some of those items are captured here.
