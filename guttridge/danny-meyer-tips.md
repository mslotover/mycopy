# Danny Meyer: Hospitality Tips

*A summary of the principles from his book Setting the Table and his talks and interviews. Built from published excerpts and summaries.*

## Who he is

Danny Meyer is the founder of Union Square Hospitality Group (Union Square Cafe, Gramercy Tavern and others) and chairman of Shake Shack. His book *Setting the Table* is widely read by restaurant managers. Its central idea is that hospitality is not the same as service. **Service is the technical delivery. Hospitality is how the guest feels during the interaction.**

## 1. Enlightened hospitality: look after people in order

Meyer ranks the people a business must care for, and the order matters:

1. **Employees**
2. **Guests**
3. **Community**
4. **Suppliers**
5. **Investors**

Each group is affected by the one before it, so care flows outward from the team. Neglect any one and the whole cycle weakens. In practice, a restaurant that looks after its staff gets better treatment of guests, which builds the community, supplier and investor relationships that follow.

## 2. Hire for the "51 percent"

His best-known hiring rule: aim for people whose skills are **51% emotional hospitality and 49% technical excellence**. He calls this a person's hospitality quotient (HQ), and defines it as their propensity to care about making other people feel better when they do their job. He says he doesn't pursue the "110-percent employee."

His reasoning is that technical skill can be taught, while a caring disposition largely can't. He puts it this way: "What's impossible to teach is how to care deeply about setting the table beautifully."

**The emotional skills he looks for** (his lists vary slightly between sources, but these recur):

| Skill | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Optimistic warmth / kind optimism** | Genuine kindness and thoughtfulness, and a sense that the glass is at least half full. Cynicism as a default emotion is a warning sign |
| **Curiosity / intelligence** | An insatiable desire to learn for its own sake. "We don't want to hire finished products" |
| **Work ethic** | A natural tendency to do something as well as it can possibly be done |
| **Empathy** | Awareness of and care for how others feel, and how your own actions make them feel |
| **Self-awareness** | Understanding what makes you tick, including your own "personal weather report" on a given day |
| **Integrity** | Having the judgment to do the right thing even when it isn't in your own interest |

**Hiring practices:**

- **Be explicit** about which emotional skills you want before you start interviewing.
- **Technical skills show quickly** (watch a cook sauté for a minute). Emotional skills take "meaningful time," often in the work environment.
- **Notice curiosity:** does the candidate ask you questions? Meyer likes waiters who want to learn about cooking, cooks who want to learn about wine, and hosts who want to learn about the guests.
- **Notice care for detail** in small acts, such as a server holding a wine glass to the light to check for smudges.
- **Ask for examples** of empathy and judgment at work, rather than yes/no questions.
- **Distinguish "I can't" from "I won't."** When a hire fails, he says the cause is more often attitude than ability. He also says most of his hiring mistakes came from misreading someone's emotional makeup.
- **Hire people for whom caring is energising.** He calls them "hospitalitarians": for them, looking after others is a kind of selfish pleasure.

## 3. Culture: name it, champion it, don't tolerate what undermines it

From a talk with 7shifts:

- **Define culture simply:** "the way we do things around here." Every business has one, whether or not anyone works at it. The question is whether it's intentional and uplifting.
- **Name the behaviours you want** to see more of, then **champion and celebrate them** when you see them.
- **Don't tolerate the opposite.** "Culture is the sum of all the behaviors that we champion minus all the wrong behaviors that we accept." Letting a toxic behaviour slide undermines everything else you say.
- **Keep core values few.** He says you can't hold people accountable for more than three to five behaviours. Define them early, without waiting until the company is big.
- **Make values behavioural.** Staff should know "not just what I'm supposed to do, but how I'm supposed to behave while I'm doing it."
- **Language is the mortar between the bricks.** Shared words for the behaviours you want make the culture stick.
- **Promote people who embody the values.** Otherwise the stated culture loses credibility.

## 4. Leadership

- **Retention is mostly about managers.** "People more often leave their manager than leave their job." In exit interviews, if you press, it comes down to leaving the boss. His answer is **servant leadership**: leaders work to serve, empower and uplift the team.
- **Manage your personal weather.** He uses the word "skunking" for letting your bad mood spill onto the team. Recognise it and deal with it before you walk in.
- **Listen to the front line.** Staff see problems approaching before leaders do. Invite specific feedback so missed standards surface early.
- **Invite criticism** and give people permission to hold you to a higher standard.
- **Surround yourself with people who want to be champions.** Know what drives you competitively: beating others, hating to lose, or beating your own best.
- **Don't believe your own press.** He is quoted as saying "The minute you start believing in your own success you are destined to fail", and that last year's wins mean you need to win more this year.
- **Stay flexible.** Some of the best businesses started as accidents. Be ready to deviate from the plan.
- **Leave things better than you found them.** This is his campsite principle from childhood summer camp.

## 5. Standards: "center the salt shaker"

Meyer's image for **constant, gentle pressure**: a saltshaker that has drifted off-centre on a table. A good leader keeps nudging it back to the agreed spot, every time, without making a fuss and without taking away anyone's dignity. He warns that pressure without gentleness can burn people out.

Related is the **excellence reflex**: the natural instinct to fix what isn't right, or improve what could be better, without waiting to be asked.

## 6. Mistakes and recovery

- **Excellence isn't the absence of mistakes.** What distinguishes a great restaurant is how well it handles them. He treats honest mistakes with patient recovery and learning, and treats failures of integrity differently.
- **Use the five As of recovery:** **awareness** (know something went wrong), **acknowledgement**, **apology**, **action** (fix it), and **additional generosity**. A business can't erase a bad experience, but it can write the next chapter.
- **Make the guest feel heard**, even when you disagree on the facts. He rejects the literal rule that "the customer is always right", but the customer must always feel heard.
- **Don't resent recurring problems.** They come like waves. Decide how you'll respond to the next one.

## 7. Making hospitality personal

- **Observe the guest,** learn what matters to them and respond thoughtfully.
- **Use his golden rule of hospitality:** "do unto others as you believe they would want done to them." Don't assume everyone wants what you'd want.
- **Gather and connect details about guests** so each visit feels known rather than processed.
- **Hospitality is a team sport.** Ask whether a candidate will look after teammates as well as guests.
- **Restaurants "are not widget factories. They are about food and love."**

## 8. Social media and storytelling

From a 2018 interview: strong social media with weak service and food only speeds up failure. What matters is what happens after people walk in. The platforms change (Michelin, Zagat, Google, now social media) but the job is still to **tell your story**, so use the channels you're comfortable with and move when your guests move.

## Notes for Amanda

- **Quick wins:** write down three to five behaviours you want, and praise them by name when you see them. Add one interview question that asks for an example of empathy. Pick one standard and "center the salt shaker" on it daily.
- **Bigger changes:** use the 51/49 lens for hiring and promotion, and track whether managers are the reason people leave.
- **Compare with Alexis Guttridge:** both emphasise small details, intentional pre-shift culture and manager empowerment. Meyer adds a stronger framework for hiring and for recovering from mistakes.
- **Caveat:** this is built from published excerpts and write-ups, not the full book. Quotes are as reported in those sources, and some come from spoken interviews, so check against *Setting the Table* before quoting him publicly.
