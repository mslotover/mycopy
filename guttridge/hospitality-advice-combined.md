## At a glance

This document combines advice from four people. **Part 1** is Alexis Guttridge, a former Boston restaurateur now advising venues in Australia, drawn from her Instagram posts. **Part 2** is Danny Meyer, founder of Union Square Hospitality Group. **Part 3** is Will Guidara, who ran Eleven Madison Park and wrote *Unreasonable Hospitality*. **Part 4** is Horst Schulze, co-founder of the Ritz-Carlton. All four reach the same conclusion: guests remember how a place made them feel, and that feeling comes from people paying attention.

**Where they agree**

- **First impressions and attention:** acknowledge every guest immediately. Alexis calls the greeting "the first course", Meyer defines hospitality as how the guest feels, and Guidara says "service is black and white; hospitality is color."
- **People first:** Meyer puts employees ahead of guests. Schulze says staff can only be excellent if leadership is. Guidara says you can't demand excellence without giving people the tools. Alexis watches how staff treat each other before she watches the guests.
- **Hire for care, teach the skills:** Meyer hires 51% on emotional skills, Guidara hires "Dream Weavers", and Alexis hires people who genuinely give a shit about people. Schulze says to select people, not just hire them.
- **Name and repeat the standards:** Meyer says champion three to five behaviours. Schulze's daily "message of the day" repeats a standard before every shift. Alexis says to replace "give great service" with specific expectations.
- **Give staff the power to fix things:** Schulze's $2,000 rule, Guidara's "start with yes" and Alexis's manager decision rights are the same idea.
- **Managers make or break it:** Meyer says people leave their manager. Alexis says to empower managers to decide without the owner.
- **Mistakes are an opportunity:** all four say what matters is how well you handle them.

**Where they differ:** Meyer and Schulze give you *systems* (hiring tests, standards, empowerment limits). Guidara gives you *generosity* (a budget for surprising guests). Alexis gives you *observation* (what to notice on the floor).

# Part 1: Alexis Guttridge


### Who she is

Alexis is a former Boston restaurateur (West Bridge). She has 20+ years of experience, sold her business at 29 and now lives in South Australia. She runs ANCHR Consulting, advising restaurants, hotels and wineries on guest experience, team culture and leadership. She also hosts the *Determined AF* podcast and speaks at industry events (for example Balancing Plates, McLaren Vale). Earlier posts (2023 to 2025) were life and business coaching for women. Her hospitality content, and the audience that came with it, began around late May 2026, and that is what this summary covers.

Her core belief appears in many posts: **guests rarely remember exactly what they ate, but they always remember how the place made them feel.**

### 1. The first 30 seconds decide the visit

- **Acknowledge every guest at the door**, even if you can't help them yet. "Hey, we'll be right with you" changes how welcome people feel. Guests left standing and unsure whether anyone noticed is, in her words, one of the most heartbreaking small moments.
- **Treat the greeting as the first course.** Your first hello has more influence than most owners realise. A warm welcome is visible: guests' shoulders drop.
- **Look at the experience before arrival.** Does anyone answer the phone? Is the booking easy? Does the outside make it obvious where to go and whether you're open? Does your Instagram match the room guests walk into? She wants the promise made online to match what people find in person.
- **Reduce confusion.** "Busy doesn't make people uncomfortable. Confusion does." Don't point, walk people there. Spot confused guests before they ask for help.
- **Give people a minute to settle** before launching into water, specials and "Have you dined with us before?" Offer somewhere to put bags, coats and flowers.

### 2. Notice the small details

Her recurring theme is that great hospitality is hundreds of tiny moments, and most cost nothing.

- **Anticipate needs:** a shell bowl with the prawns, napkins with the ribs, a steak knife before the steak. Clear space on the table before the next dish arrives.
- **Never let a glass sit empty** or a table go uncleared. A great bartender spots a nearly empty glass with a nod, not a wave.
- **Glassware and cutlery matter.** Water spots, a glass still hot from the dishwasher and poor cutlery all register with guests.
- **Make information travel with the guest.** The allergy, the birthday and the early finish should never need repeating. "A guest shouldn't have to tell their story three times."
- **Let each table set its own pace.** Some want to linger, others have a show to catch.
- **Remember something personal**, such as the holiday they mentioned or the dog, rather than just their drink order.
- **Say goodbye as warmly as hello.** The last 30 seconds matter. Use a thank-you, eye contact and a name, not a cheesy script.
- **Check the room itself:** sound levels (can people hear each other?), lighting, music, bathrooms, the "table nobody wants" and the menu's clarity. Make someone specifically responsible for bathroom checks and table clearing, rather than "someone should".

### 3. Build regulars from the first visit

"Regulars aren't made on the tenth visit. They're made on the first." First-time guests are far more observant than regulars, so use their first impression well. Recommendations:

- Remember names, favourite seats and favourite drinks.
- Make people feel like regulars before they are, through genuine recognition rather than a loyalty scheme.
- Introduce new staff to regulars. Good staff introduce themselves rather than wait to be recognised.
- Treat regulars as people who are home, not VIPs. The best ones don't expect special treatment.
- Look after solo diners and bar guests. A table for one is not a sad table. Check on them once more and offer conversation if they want it.

### 4. Leadership and managers

- **Managers should be on the floor, not behind a laptop.** "You cannot lead hospitality from behind a screen." The best managers run food, clear tables and seat guests.
- **Make managers able to decide without the owner.** Ask "What do you think we should do?" and "Come to me with the problem AND what you think the solution is." Great managers "don't create followers. They create more people who can lead."
- **Test the system:** "What happens to the experience when your best manager isn't there?" If the answer is "it falls apart", that is the first thing to work on.
- **Beware of owner dependence.** She says she over-controlled at West Bridge and that burnout taught her the difference between caring about everything and being involved in everything. "You can give everything to this industry without giving it every single piece of you."
- **Make it safe to admit mistakes.** Teach staff to say "I screwed this up, I know how to fix it, I'm on it." Mistakes get bigger when people are scared to report them.
- **Recognise what's working.** Name good behaviour when you see it, whether that is someone going out of their way for a guest or stepping in to help a teammate. That is how it becomes part of how the restaurant operates, instead of one good employee's habit. Give the team both: "here's what we're doing really well" and "here's where we can get better."
- **Don't carry one difficult guest into the next table.** Fix what you can, learn what's worth learning, then give the next guest the same version of you.

### 5. Pre-shift (lineup)

She treats pre-shift as one of the highest-leverage routines.

- Use it to remind the team what kind of experience they are about to create, not just to pass on specials and bookings.
- Don't turn every lineup into a list of yesterday's mistakes. Include something to feel good about.
- **Teach one thing:** a dish, a wine, how to greet a guest, a question for servers to ask that night.
- **Set the energy.** Think game day. If you can't bring energy that day, let someone else run it.
- **Prepare.** Take five minutes beforehand to decide what you want the team to know, feel or do differently.
- Tell the team what to look for: empty glasses, guests waiting at the door, tables that need clearing.
- Pick one dish or drink and have everyone describe it without reading the menu.
- Ask: "What's one tiny thing we could do tonight that a guest might still remember tomorrow?"

### 6. Team culture and consistency

- **Watch how staff treat each other**, not just how they treat guests. It is one of the fastest ways to read culture. After the last guest leaves, see whether people say goodnight to the kitchen, thank the dishwasher and check on the bartender.
- **Set specific standards.** "Give great service" isn't training. Replace "make guests feel welcome" with "acknowledge every guest who walks through the door, even if you can't help them immediately."
- **Check consistency across shifts and managers.** Pick one piece of the experience (the greeting, check-backs, farewells) and watch it on different shifts.
- **Ask who owns the floor** when service goes sideways, so the strongest person doesn't end up putting out every fire.
- **Spot the person carrying the shift.** One exhausted staff member quietly fixing everyone's mistakes is a warning sign of culture breaking down.
- **Hire for who people are**, not only what they've done. Some of the best hospitality people weren't the most experienced, but they genuinely gave a shit about people. She advises developing natural talent by letting them lead a pre-shift, recommend a wine or own a guest experience, and telling them what they did well and why it mattered.

### 7. Diagnose causes, not symptoms

A frequent message to owners: trace a complaint backwards before fixing it.

| What you hear | Where she'd look first |
|---|---|
| "Staff keep leaving" | Who they work for, how they're managed and their first 30 days |
| "Service is inconsistent" | Whether expectations are clear, whether there is a proper pre-shift, and whether training is followed up |
| "Managers are burning out" | Which decisions they're actually allowed to make |
| "Guests aren't coming back" | The whole guest experience, from arrival to departure and follow-up |
| "The owner can't step away" | Why one person still makes every decision |
| "It feels chaotic" | Reservation clumping, nowhere to wait, door management, server loads, and the manager covering three roles |
| "We need more customers" | Often a better reason for existing guests to return |
| "We think it's staffing" | Often a leadership and communication problem |

**Hidden costs:** lost knowledge when good staff leave, managers fighting fires, repeated conversations and owner dependence all hurt the bottom line without appearing on the P&L.

**For slow nights:** pick one problem, such as dead Tuesdays or occasion-only guests. Ask what would give existing guests a reason to come, and talk to five people who already love you. Stop protecting something just because it used to work.

### 8. How she assesses a venue (steal this)

Her method is a useful checklist for any owner:

1. **Experience it as a guest first.** Call, book online, and check Google, the website and Instagram.
2. **Look for patterns in reviews**, not individual complaints. Six people describing the same frustration over six months is the signal.
3. **Ask people outside the business** what they've heard, or pay a trusted friend to dine and report back.
4. **Have an anonymous dinner** on an ordinary night.
5. **Look upstream.** Slow service, a server who can't answer menu questions and a frantic team may all trace back to training, permissions or systems.
6. **Watch the managers** under pressure. Who does the team look at when things go sideways?
7. **Understand why things are the way they are** before changing anything. She says a venue deserves more than "someone with a clipboard and a list of everything you're doing wrong".

She has built her **Client Audit** around this, and offers a **free 25-area self-assessment tool** (comment "LOOK" on her posts). Her 21-point audit series ends on one question: *"Would I trust you with someone I care about?"*

### 9. Getting fresh eyes on your own venue

If you've walked in 10,000 times, it's hard to see. She suggests asking another owner to walk through and tell you what you're not seeing, visiting somewhere very different, following non-competitors such as wineries and hotels, and asking your team what one thing they'd change. An outside advisor is another option, but the point is the perspective, not who provides it.

### 10. Balance and sustainability

She is open about burnout and argues that the industry's intensity shouldn't cost you your health or your life. Don't let the restaurant always come first, prefer "better, not bigger" growth, and keep learning. She also asks guests to be gracious: tell an owner privately before posting a damaging review, thank staff, stay off your phone when the server arrives, and say specifically what you loved.


# Part 2: Danny Meyer


### Who he is

Danny Meyer is the founder of Union Square Hospitality Group (Union Square Cafe, Gramercy Tavern and others) and chairman of Shake Shack. His book *Setting the Table* is widely read by restaurant managers. Its central idea is that hospitality is not the same as service. **Service is the technical delivery. Hospitality is how the guest feels during the interaction.**

### 1. Enlightened hospitality: look after people in order

Meyer ranks the people a business must care for, and the order matters:

1. **Employees**
2. **Guests**
3. **Community**
4. **Suppliers**
5. **Investors**

Each group is affected by the one before it, so care flows outward from the team. Neglect any one and the whole cycle weakens. In practice, a restaurant that looks after its staff gets better treatment of guests, which builds the community, supplier and investor relationships that follow.

### 2. Hire for the "51 percent"

His best-known hiring rule: aim for people whose skills are **51% emotional hospitality and 49% technical excellence**. He calls this a person's hospitality quotient (HQ), and defines it as their propensity to care about making other people feel better when they do their job. He says he doesn't pursue the "110-percent employee."

His reasoning is that technical skill can be taught, while a caring disposition largely can't. He puts it this way: "What's impossible to teach is how to care deeply about setting the table beautifully."

**The emotional skills he looks for** (his lists vary slightly between sources, but these recur):

| Skill | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Optimistic warmth / kind optimism** | Genuine kindness and thoughtfulness, and a sense that the glass is at least half full. Cynicism as a default emotion is a warning sign |
| **Curiosity / intelligence** | An insatiable desire to learn for its own sake. "We don't want to hire finished products" |
| **Work ethic** | A natural tendency to do something as well as it can possibly be done |
| **Empathy** | Awareness of and care for how others feel, and how your own actions make them feel |
| **Self-awareness** | Understanding what makes you tick, including your own "personal weather report" on a given day |
| **Integrity** | Having the judgment to do the right thing even when it isn't in your own interest |

**Hiring practices:**

- **Be explicit** about which emotional skills you want before you start interviewing.
- **Technical skills show quickly** (watch a cook sauté for a minute). Emotional skills take "meaningful time," often in the work environment.
- **Notice curiosity:** does the candidate ask you questions? Meyer likes waiters who want to learn about cooking, cooks who want to learn about wine, and hosts who want to learn about the guests.
- **Notice care for detail** in small acts, such as a server holding a wine glass to the light to check for smudges.
- **Ask for examples** of empathy and judgment at work, rather than yes/no questions.
- **Distinguish "I can't" from "I won't."** When a hire fails, he says the cause is more often attitude than ability. He also says most of his hiring mistakes came from misreading someone's emotional makeup.
- **Hire people for whom caring is energising.** He calls them "hospitalitarians": for them, looking after others is a kind of selfish pleasure.

### 3. Culture: name it, champion it, don't tolerate what undermines it

From a talk with 7shifts:

- **Define culture simply:** "the way we do things around here." Every business has one, whether or not anyone works at it. The question is whether it's intentional and uplifting.
- **Name the behaviours you want** to see more of, then **champion and celebrate them** when you see them.
- **Don't tolerate the opposite.** "Culture is the sum of all the behaviors that we champion minus all the wrong behaviors that we accept." Letting a toxic behaviour slide undermines everything else you say.
- **Keep core values few.** He says you can't hold people accountable for more than three to five behaviours. Define them early, without waiting until the company is big.
- **Make values behavioural.** Staff should know "not just what I'm supposed to do, but how I'm supposed to behave while I'm doing it."
- **Language is the mortar between the bricks.** Shared words for the behaviours you want make the culture stick.
- **Promote people who embody the values.** Otherwise the stated culture loses credibility.

### 4. Leadership

- **Retention is mostly about managers.** "People more often leave their manager than leave their job." In exit interviews, if you press, it comes down to leaving the boss. His answer is **servant leadership**: leaders work to serve, empower and uplift the team.
- **Manage your personal weather.** He uses the word "skunking" for letting your bad mood spill onto the team. Recognise it and deal with it before you walk in.
- **Listen to the front line.** Staff see problems approaching before leaders do. Invite specific feedback so missed standards surface early.
- **Invite criticism** and give people permission to hold you to a higher standard.
- **Surround yourself with people who want to be champions.** Know what drives you competitively: beating others, hating to lose, or beating your own best.
- **Don't believe your own press.** He is quoted as saying "The minute you start believing in your own success you are destined to fail", and that last year's wins mean you need to win more this year.
- **Stay flexible.** Some of the best businesses started as accidents. Be ready to deviate from the plan.
- **Leave things better than you found them.** This is his campsite principle from childhood summer camp.

### 5. Standards: "center the salt shaker"

Meyer's image for **constant, gentle pressure**: a saltshaker that has drifted off-centre on a table. A good leader keeps nudging it back to the agreed spot, every time, without making a fuss and without taking away anyone's dignity. He warns that pressure without gentleness can burn people out.

Related is the **excellence reflex**: the natural instinct to fix what isn't right, or improve what could be better, without waiting to be asked.

### 6. Mistakes and recovery

- **Excellence isn't the absence of mistakes.** What distinguishes a great restaurant is how well it handles them. He treats honest mistakes with patient recovery and learning, and treats failures of integrity differently.
- **Use the five As of recovery:** **awareness** (know something went wrong), **acknowledgement**, **apology**, **action** (fix it), and **additional generosity**. A business can't erase a bad experience, but it can write the next chapter.
- **Make the guest feel heard**, even when you disagree on the facts. He rejects the literal rule that "the customer is always right", but the customer must always feel heard.
- **Don't resent recurring problems.** They come like waves. Decide how you'll respond to the next one.

### 7. Making hospitality personal

- **Observe the guest,** learn what matters to them and respond thoughtfully.
- **Use his golden rule of hospitality:** "do unto others as you believe they would want done to them." Don't assume everyone wants what you'd want.
- **Gather and connect details about guests** so each visit feels known rather than processed.
- **Hospitality is a team sport.** Ask whether a candidate will look after teammates as well as guests.
- **Restaurants "are not widget factories. They are about food and love."**

### 8. Social media and storytelling

From a 2018 interview: strong social media with weak service and food only speeds up failure. What matters is what happens after people walk in. The platforms change (Michelin, Zagat, Google, now social media) but the job is still to **tell your story**, so use the channels you're comfortable with and move when your guests move.


# Part 3: Will Guidara

*Based on secondary summaries of his book* Unreasonable Hospitality, *his TED talk and interviews.*

### Who he is

Will Guidara started at Danny Meyer's Union Square Hospitality Group. He later co-ran Eleven Madison Park with chef Daniel Humm and took it to the top of the World's 50 Best list in 2017. His book argues that any business serving people should offer "unreasonable hospitality", meaning giving people more than they expect.

### 1. Hospitality versus service

- "Service is black and white; hospitality is color." Service is delivering the product. Hospitality is how that delivery makes the person feel.
- "Don't just serve them dinner; serve them memories."
- "One size fits one." Tailor to the individual, not the average guest.
- "Hospitality is a dialogue, not a monologue." Listen to the guest and respond.
- "Hospitality is a selfish pleasure." Giving also feeds the giver, which echoes Meyer's "hospitalitarians".
- Anyone can choose to be in the hospitality business, whatever their job.

### 2. The 95/5 rule: budget for generosity

- Manage 95% of the business down to the penny, then spend the last 5% "foolishly" on generosity. "If you aren't managing the 95%, you'll never have the money for the 5%."
- "Generosity is an investment, not an expense."
- **The hot dog story:** Guidara overheard guests saying they hadn't tried a New York hot dog. He ran out, bought one, and had the chef plate it beautifully. The lesson is to **listen for what isn't being asked for**, then deliver it. The idea came from frontline staff, not the owner.
- **Systematise your kindness.** One example is looking up a guest in advance so you can personalise their visit.

### 3. Creativity and "yes"

- "Start with the answer 'Yes', and work backward."
- "Excellence is the accumulation of thousands of details."
- "No one who ever changed the game did so by being reasonable."
- Creativity is an active process. At Eleven Madison Park, "Dream Weaver" meetings brainstormed ideas for guests.
- Slowing the team down now builds a stronger foundation for later speed.

### 4. Culture and hiring

- **Hire "Dream Weavers":** people who take joy in making others happy. Skills can be taught, caring can't.
- "Culture can't be taught; it has to be caught."
- **Language creates culture.** Examples are saying "opportunities" for problems and "feedback" for complaints.
- A daily 30-minute team meeting is described as the best lever for culture change.
- "The best way to respect and reward the A players on your team is to surround them with other A players."
- Make sure people who are trying have what they need to succeed.

### 5. Leadership

- **Praise in public, criticise in private.** Praise with emotion, criticise without it, and stick to facts.
- "Criticize the behavior, not the person." "You were late" is a fact. "You are irresponsible" is an attack.
- "Manage the person, not the title."
- "Sometimes the best time to promote people is before they are ready."
- "Consistency is one of the most important and underrated aspects of being a leader."
- "You cannot demand excellence from your team if you are not giving them the tools to be excellent", and you must hold them to the standards you set.
- "Trust is the currency of leadership." Without it you end up verifying everything, which costs speed and money.
- Earn trust by being human with your team.

### 6. Mistakes and adversity

- "You're going to mess up. When you do, apologize."
- "Adversity is a terrible thing to waste." Use a crisis to fix systems and bring the team together.
- Take the work seriously, but not yourself.

# Part 4: Horst Schulze

*Based on interviews and write-ups about his book* Excellence Wins *(Wharton, Corporate Board Member, Chief Executive, Hospitality Net).*

### Who he is

Horst Schulze was a co-founder and long-time president of the Ritz-Carlton hotel company, famous for its service culture. His credo is "We are ladies and gentlemen serving ladies and gentlemen."

### 1. Empower every employee to fix problems

- **The $2,000 rule:** any employee could spend up to $2,000 to resolve a guest's problem and keep them. Staff weren't to send guests to a manager. The response was "Please forgive me, I'll take care of it", and "never lose a guest."
- **The economics:** by his reasoning, a repeat guest was worth around $200,000 over a lifetime, so spending to keep one was "an economic decision."
- **In practice:** "Nobody ever spent $2,000, but they bought breakfast or sent a food basket or some cookies." One example: a guest kept awake by street noise got breakfast bought for them. Staff were trained in "proper resolution" before they were trusted with it.
- Another commentator notes that the budget isn't the point. You need to hire people with good judgment and train them to use it.

### 2. A daily lineup: "message of the day"

- Before every shift, staff heard one of the company's standards (he describes about 20 things that make the company better than competitors). "You couldn't go to work unless you had the message of the day."
- The same message came back roughly every 20 days. He describes training as "teaching and repeating and sustaining."

### 3. Treat staff as ladies and gentlemen

- The credo means respect for employees, though he says they keep that respect only if they show they deserve it.
- "Don't come to work to work ever. Come to work to create excellence."
- Staff were expected to give opinions. One manager reportedly quit rather than listen to employees' views. Schulze says turnover stayed low without paying a premium.

### 4. Select, don't just hire

- "Not just hire people but select people." Recruit by inviting them into a mission: "Join me, don't come to work here."
- Orientation brings people into the company's thinking and gives them a purpose, not just a job.

### 5. Define quality and chase mistakes

- Excellence is individualisation: delivering what *this* customer wants, "do it my way."
- "Elegance without warmth is arrogance."
- **Eliminate mistakes rather than cut costs.** Fixing a mistake permanently saves money and improves the product at once. "Cost-cutting means you take something away from the customer", and "Any fool can do cost-cutting."
- Keep existing guests first, then win new ones, then get more value from the guests you have.

### 6. Leadership

- Employees can only be excellent if leadership is excellent.
- Managers must help staff succeed and welcome their opinions. Every employee should know their objective is to bring the guest back.

# Where to start

- **Quick wins, all free:** acknowledge every arrival, run a tighter pre-shift with one standard repeated (Schulze's "message of the day"), name three to five behaviours you want and praise them when you see them, and assign owners for bathrooms and table clearing.
- **Empowerment:** decide a spending limit staff can use on the spot to fix a guest problem, and tell them. It will almost never be used in full.
- **A generosity budget:** after you've got the numbers under control, set aside a small share (Guidara's 5%) for gestures that come from listening to guests.
- **Hiring:** add an interview question that asks for an example of empathy, and watch candidates in the work environment where you can.
- **Management:** work out which decisions managers can't make without you, and ask what happens when your best manager isn't there. Praise in public and correct in private.
- **Diagnosis:** before fixing a complaint, trace it backwards. Alexis's free 25-area assessment tool and a quiet guest-eyes visit are good first steps.
- **Caveats:** Part 1 is based on Instagram captions only, so her video and carousel detail is only partly captured. Parts 2 to 4 are based on published excerpts, interviews and write-ups rather than the full books, so check quotes against *Setting the Table*, *Unreasonable Hospitality* and *Excellence Wins* before using them publicly. Some of Guidara's and Schulze's quotes come from spoken interviews or secondary summaries.
